Design document · living draft

40 is a payment risk engine that notices when you're being pressured, not just when a transaction looks odd.

Most fraud detection watches the transaction. 40 also watches the moment around it — a new device, an active call, an unfamiliar payee, a rushed high-value transfer — and steps in with a warning before money moves, without getting in the way of a payment you actually meant to make.

Stage: architecture & data design
Core idea: on-device scoring, distilled from a heavier server-side model
Status: 5 open decisions below
Why this exists

The problem

Coercion-based fraud — a scammer on the phone walking a victim through an "urgent" transfer — doesn't look anomalous by the numbers most fraud systems watch. The amount can be normal. The account can be the victim's own, verified, familiar. What's different is the situation the person is in when they hit send.

What existing systems catch

Statistical outliers: an unusual amount, an unfamiliar payee, a spike in transaction frequency. Well-suited to card-fraud patterns, less suited to a victim knowingly authorizing a payment under pressure.

What 40 tries to add

Situational signals around the transaction — device change, an active call, urgency cues — combined with transaction anomalies, to catch the pattern of coercion, not just the pattern of the payment itself.

⚠️

Named tension, not yet resolved: the same design goal — warn on coercion without blocking legitimate urgent payments — is the classic hard problem in fraud systems. A threshold tight enough to catch real coercion will also flag some genuine emergencies. See What we're uncertain about.

Where we stand

Current understanding

What we currently believe, in plain terms, before any code is written.

A transaction carries two kinds of signal
One is what is being paid — amount, payee, frequency, account history. The other is how it's being paid — from a new or unfamiliar device, while on an active call, unusually fast after the payee was added. 40 is built on the belief that the second kind matters as much as the first for catching coercion specifically.
Scoring has to happen close to the user, not just in a data center
A warning that arrives after the money has left isn't a warning. This pushes toward on-device or near-device inference — fast, and without needing to ship raw behavioral data off the phone for every single transaction.
A warning is not a block
The system's job is to interrupt and inform, then let the person decide. This is a deliberate choice — blocking legitimate urgent payments causes its own harm, and erodes trust in the system fast enough that people start ignoring or disabling it.
💡

We don't yet have real labeled coercion data — no dataset tags a transaction as "victim was being coerced." That signal is being constructed, not observed, from documented scam patterns. This is addressed directly in Data strategy rather than glossed over.

The plan

Our approach

A teacher-student split: a heavy, unsupervised model does the hard work of finding structure in transaction and behavioral data once, offline. A small model learns to imitate its judgment, cheaply, in real time, on the device.

Teacher — server side

An Isolation Forest + Autoencoder ensemble trained on a merged, feature-engineered dataset. Produces an anomaly score per transaction. Runs offline, not in the real-time path.

Student — on device

A small model (compact MLP or shallow tree) trained to reproduce the teacher's scores using only the features available locally on the phone. Runs in the real-time path, at the moment of payment.

The teacher is retrained periodically as institutions report back which flagged transactions were real fraud and which were false alarms. A new student is distilled from the updated teacher and shipped to devices. Assumption — the exact shipping mechanism (in-app update vs. background model download) hasn't been decided; see open items.

Walkthrough

How it works

Three phases, from the moment a payment is initiated to the moment the system gets a little smarter because of it.

1
On-device detection
Every transaction, scored locally, in real time
1
Local feature extraction
At the moment a payment is initiated, the device computes transaction features (amount, payee, frequency) alongside situational signals — is this a new device, is there an active call, has this payee only just been added. Assumption device- and call-state signals require explicit permission at onboarding; this consent flow is not yet designed. See Q3.
2
The student model scores it
The distilled on-device model produces a risk score from those features, without a network call, in well under the time a payment normally takes to process.
2
Warning & confirmation
Only when risk crosses a threshold
3
A plain-language warning appears
Not a generic "suspicious activity" message — a specific reason tied to what triggered the score, e.g. "new device + call in progress + first-time payee." Open the exact method for surfacing this explanation from a distilled model hasn't been chosen; see Q1 region.
4
The person decides
Confirm and the payment proceeds. Cancel and it doesn't. The system never makes this decision unilaterally — it interrupts, it doesn't override.
3
Institutional review & retraining
The feedback loop that makes the model better over time
5
Flagged cases reach a review queue
Bank or institution staff mark whether a flagged transaction was genuine fraud, genuine coercion, or a false positive. Open the review dashboard itself is not yet designed as a component — see Q5.
6
The teacher retrains, a new student ships
Labeled outcomes feed the next teacher retraining run. A freshly distilled student model is generated and rolled out to devices on some cadence. Assumption cadence and rollout mechanism undecided.
Grounding the model

Data strategy

Three real, public fraud datasets are merged into one schema. What none of them contain — coercion and device-behavior signals — is added synthetically, deliberately, and disclosed rather than hidden.

SourceReal or syntheticContributesMissing
ULB Credit Card FraudReal, labeledClean fraud/not-fraud labels, amount, timingNo device or behavioral fields
IEEE-CIS Fraud DetectionReal, labeledDevice type, browser, card metadataNo call-state or coercion-specific signal
PaySimSyntheticMobile-money-style payment flows, balancesNo device or behavioral fields
Coercion augmentationSynthetic, rule-basedCall-active, device-new, urgency flagsNot observed data — constructed from documented scam patterns
⚠️

What this means honestly: the unsupervised teacher model isn't learning "what coercion looks like" from real examples — it's learning to flag combinations of signals that we, based on documented scam patterns, decided should count as coercion-like. That's a meaningfully different and more limited claim than "the model learned to detect coercion," and the site is stating it plainly rather than implying otherwise.

Merging three heterogeneous sources risks a model that learns which dataset a row came from rather than genuine fraud signal. A source-prediction sanity check — training a simple classifier to guess origin dataset from features — is planned as a validation step before trusting the merged data. Open not yet run.

Established

What we've figured out

Warn, don't block

Confirmed direction — the system interrupts and informs, the person decides.

Teacher-student split

Confirmed architecture — heavy unsupervised model offline, light distilled model on-device.

Real datasets as a base

ULB, IEEE-CIS and PaySim identified as the grounding data, with a defined merge schema.

Synthetic data is disclosed

Coercion signals are constructed, not observed — and the design commits to saying so, not hiding it.

Not yet settled

What we're uncertain about

Named directly rather than smoothed over — these are the gaps that would change the design if resolved differently.

A

What exactly crosses the device-to-institution boundary

Raw transaction, risk score only, or score plus a feature summary — undecided, and it's the crux of whether "privacy-preserving" is a real property of this system.

B

Consent for device and call-state signals

These require OS-level permissions distinct from transaction-data consent, and no flow for obtaining them has been designed yet.

C

Threshold governance

Who sets the line between "annoying false positive" and "missed real coercion," whether it's global or adaptive per user, and how it gets tuned — not yet defined.

D

On-device explainability

The teacher can be explained with SHAP; the distilled on-device student is smaller and less inherently interpretable. How the plain-language warning gets generated from it is unresolved.

E

Model update mechanism

How an updated student model actually reaches a device — app-store release, background download, something else — hasn't been decided, and each option has different security implications.

Named, not solved

Risks

Adversarial adaptation
A scammer who learns the system's triggers can coach a victim around them — "don't mention you're on a call," "wait a day before transferring." Fraud models degrade against an adaptive adversary; this is acknowledged as an ongoing risk, not a solved problem.
Data source leakage
Merging three heterogeneous datasets risks the model learning dataset-origin artifacts instead of genuine fraud patterns. A validation check is planned but not yet run.
Regulatory alignment unverified
Existing regulatory guidance on fraud alerting for financial institutions likely applies here and hasn't been reviewed against this design yet.
What's next

Roadmap

NOW

Resolve the boundary question

Decide what data actually leaves the device on a flagged transaction — this gates most of the rest of the build.

NEXT

Build the merged dataset + run the leakage check

Unify ULB, IEEE-CIS, PaySim into one schema, backfill coercion signals, validate the merge before trusting it.

THEN

Train teacher, distill student, benchmark on-device

Get real numbers on model size and inference latency — these become concrete claims, not estimates.

LATER

Design the warning UI and the review queue

Both are real components, not afterthoughts — the warning is the entire user-facing product, and the review queue is what makes the feedback loop real.
